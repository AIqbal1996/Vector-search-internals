# Viva Voce Questions & Answers: Vector Search Internals

Comprehensive preparation guide containing 35 oral examination questions with simple, accurate, and confident explanations tailored for academic defense.

---

### 1. What is a vector?
**Answer:** A vector is an ordered sequence of numerical values (coordinates) representing a point or magnitude and direction in a multi-dimensional geometric space. For example, a 2D vector $[2, 3]$ represents coordinates $(x=2, y=3)$. In machine learning, vectors represent features or semantic embeddings in hundreds or thousands of dimensions.

---

### 2. What is an embedding?
**Answer:** An embedding is a dense, continuous numerical vector generated by a deep neural network (such as Google's Gemini Embedding 2) that translates complex unstructured data (text, images, audio) into geometric space such that semantically similar concepts are positioned close to one another.

---

### 3. What is Euclidean distance?
**Answer:** Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between two points in Euclidean space, calculated as the square root of the sum of squared differences across all dimensions:
$$d(a, b) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^D (a_i - b_i)^2}$$
A distance of $0$ indicates identical points. Smaller values indicate greater proximity.

---

### 4. What is cosine similarity?
**Answer:** Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between two non-zero vectors:
$$\text{cosine\_sim}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\| \|b\|}$$
It ranges from $-1.0$ (opposite directions) to $1.0$ (identical direction). It measures directional orientation completely independent of vector length or magnitude.

---

### 5. What is the dot product?
**Answer:** The dot product (or scalar product) is the sum of the products of corresponding components:
$$a \cdot b = \sum_{i=1}^D a_i b_i = \|a\| \|b\| \cos \theta$$
It combines both directional alignment ($\cos \theta$) and vector magnitudes ($\|a\| \times \|b\|$).

---

### 6. Why do Euclidean distance and Cosine similarity rankings differ?
**Answer:** Because Euclidean distance measures physical translation distance in coordinate space, whereas Cosine similarity measures angular alignment ignoring magnitude.
In our classroom example, candidate $B(1,2)$ is collinear with query $Q(2,4)$ because $Q = 2 \times B$. Because they point along the exact same ray ($\theta = 0^\circ$), Point B achieves a perfect Cosine score of $1.0$, even though it is physically further in Euclidean distance ($\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$) than Point $A(2,3)$ ($d = 1.0$).

---

### 7. What is vector normalization?
**Answer:** Vector normalization (specifically $L_2$ normalization) is the process of dividing a vector by its Euclidean length (norm):
$$\hat{v} = \frac{v}{\|v\|_2}$$
This scales the vector so that its length becomes exactly $1.0$, projecting it onto the surface of a unit hypersphere without altering its spatial direction.

---

### 8. Why does the normalized dot product equal cosine similarity?
**Answer:** By definition:
$$\text{cosine\_sim}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\| \|b\|}$$
When vectors are $L_2$-normalized, their magnitudes are $\|\hat{a}\| = 1$ and $\|\hat{b}\| = 1$. Substituting these into the formula:
$$\text{cosine\_sim}(\hat{a}, \hat{b}) = \frac{\hat{a} \cdot \hat{b}}{1 \times 1} = \hat{a} \cdot \hat{b}$$
Thus, on unit vectors, cosine similarity is identical to the dot product.

---

### 9. What is the mathematical relationship between Euclidean distance and Cosine similarity on unit vectors?
**Answer:** For any two unit vectors $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{b}$:
$$\|\hat{a} - \hat{b}\|^2 = \|\hat{a}\|^2 + \|\hat{b}\|^2 - 2(\hat{a} \cdot \hat{b}) = 1 + 1 - 2\cos\theta = 2 - 2\cos\theta$$
$$d_{\text{euclid}}(\hat{a}, \hat{b}) = \sqrt{2 - 2 \cdot \text{cosine\_sim}(a, b)}$$
Because $\sqrt{2 - 2x}$ is strictly monotonically decreasing, minimizing Euclidean distance on unit vectors yields the exact same ranking as maximizing Cosine similarity.

---

### 10. What is brute-force vector search?
**Answer:** Brute-force (or flat/exact) vector search compares the query vector against every single vector in the database, calculates the exact distance/similarity score for each, and sorts them to find the top-$k$ nearest neighbors.

---

### 11. What is the time complexity of brute-force search?
**Answer:** Computing distances takes $O(N \cdot D)$, where $N$ is the number of database vectors and $D$ is the embedding dimension. Selecting top-$k$ takes $O(N \log k)$ using a partial sort (like `np.argpartition`). Thus, overall complexity is $O(N \cdot D + N \log k)$.

---

### 12. What is top-k search?
**Answer:** Top-$k$ search returns the $k$ most relevant (closest) vectors to the query out of the entire database, rather than returning all candidates. Common values for $k$ in RAG pipelines are $3, 5, 10,$ or $20$.

---

### 13. What is Qdrant?
**Answer:** Qdrant is an open-source, high-performance vector search engine and database written in Rust. It offers production-grade vector similarity search with rich payload filtering, distributed clustering, and HNSW graph indexing.

---

### 14. What is HNSW?
**Answer:** HNSW stands for **Hierarchical Navigable Small World**. It is a graph-based Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) indexing algorithm that organizes vectors into multi-layer graphs (similar to a skip-list), enabling fast nearest neighbor search with expected $O(\log N)$ complexity.

---

### 15. What is Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search?
**Answer:** ANN search trades a small, controlled amount of retrieval precision (e.g., retrieving 98% of the true top-$k$) in exchange for orders of magnitude faster search latency compared to exhaustive brute-force search.

---

### 16. What is the difference between Exact Search and Approximate Search?
**Answer:** Exact search guarantees 100% recall by evaluating every candidate, but scales linearly $O(N)$. Approximate search navigates an index (like HNSW) to examine only a small fraction of candidates, scaling logarithmically $O(\log N)$, but may occasionally miss a true nearest neighbor.

---

### 17. What is Recall@k?
**Answer:** Recall@k measures the fraction of true nearest neighbors (as identified by exact brute force) that are successfully retrieved by an approximate search algorithm:
$$\text{Recall@k} = \frac{|\text{Top-}k_{\text{exact}} \cap \text{Top-}k_{\text{approx}}|}{k}$$
A Recall@10 of $1.0$ (100%) means all 10 true nearest neighbors were retrieved.

---

### 18. Why can NumPy be faster than Qdrant for 1,000 vectors?
**Answer:** For 1,000 vectors, vectorized NumPy matrix multiplication runs directly in CPU L1/L2 cache and memory using SIMD instructions in less than $1$ millisecond. In contrast, calling Qdrant Cloud requires serialization, network transit over HTTPS, TLS handshakes, and deserialization, which incurs a $40$ to $80$ ms network round-trip overhead.

---

### 19. When does HNSW become beneficial?
**Answer:** HNSW becomes indispensable at scale—typically when $N \ge 100,000$ to millions or billions of vectors. At that scale, linear brute force takes seconds or minutes, while HNSW maintains sub-10ms search latency.

---

### 20. What is the role of parameter `M` in HNSW?
**Answer:** $M$ defines the maximum number of bi-directional links (connections) each vector node maintains per layer. Higher $M$ improves recall and graph connectivity on complex data, but increases index build time and RAM consumption.

---

### 21. What is the role of parameter `ef_construct` in HNSW?
**Answer:** `ef_construct` controls the size of the dynamic candidate list evaluated while building the HNSW graph. Higher `ef_construct` leads to higher quality graph construction and better recall, but increases index build time. It does not affect search-time RAM.

---

### 22. What is the role of parameter `hnsw_ef` at search time?
**Answer:** `hnsw_ef` (or `ef_search`) specifies the size of the dynamic priority queue used during Layer 0 search traversal. Increasing `hnsw_ef` improves Recall@k at the expense of slightly higher query latency. It can be tuned dynamically per query without rebuilding the index.

---

### 23. What happens when embedding dimensions increase (the Curse of Dimensionality)?
**Answer:** In very high dimensions, the distance between any two random vectors concentrates around a narrow distribution (distances become equidistant), and spatial volume grows exponentially. This increases the computation per vector ($O(D)$) and makes geometric partitioning harder.

---

### 24. Why do we store embeddings as `float32`?
**Answer:** `float32` (single-precision floating point, 4 bytes per number) provides the optimal balance of numerical precision (7 decimal digits) and hardware acceleration (AVX/SIMD instructions on CPUs and GPUs). `float64` doubles memory consumption with no meaningful gain in semantic retrieval accuracy.

---

### 25. What is a zero vector and how should it be handled?
**Answer:** A zero vector has all components equal to zero ($[0, 0, \dots, 0]$), meaning $\|v\| = 0$. Cosine similarity is mathematically undefined for zero vectors due to division by zero. Robust systems explicitly check for zero norms and return a default similarity of $0.0$ or raise a validation exception.

---

### 26. How are tied scores handled in your implementation?
**Answer:** When two vectors have identical similarity or distance scores within floating-point tolerance, we break ties deterministically using the unique integer record ID in ascending order (implemented via `np.lexsort`). This guarantees reproducible, deterministic ranking.

---

### 27. What is P95 and P99 latency?
**Answer:** P95 latency is the 95th percentile query latency: 95% of queries execute faster than this value, and only the slowest 5% exceed it. P99 represents the 99th percentile (worst 1%). Percentiles reflect realistic user experience better than the mean, which can be distorted by outliers.

---

### 28. What is index construction overhead?
**Answer:** Building an index like HNSW requires evaluating distances between newly inserted vectors and existing nodes to wire edges ($O(N \log N)$ complexity). This upfront compute and additional memory overhead is the trade-off for achieving fast query times later.

---

### 29. How did you protect API credentials in this project?
**Answer:** API credentials (`EURI_API_KEY`, `QDRANT_API_KEY`) are stored exclusively in an uncommitted local `.env` file, which is listed in `.gitignore`. A `.env.example` file contains empty placeholders for safe repository distribution. No secret is ever printed to terminal output or committed to Git.

### 30. How did you validate that NumPy and Qdrant produced identical results?
**Answer:** We executed identical queries against both engines and compared:
1. Top-$k$ record IDs
2. Ranking order
3. Numerical scores (with absolute tolerance of $10^{-4}$)
4. Recall@10 (achieved 100.00% match)
5. Exported a full comparison report to `results/comparison_results.json`.

### 32. What is the difference between real embeddings and synthetic benchmark vectors?
**Answer:** Real embeddings are generated from natural text via neural network inference and carry genuine semantic semantic clusters. Synthetic benchmark vectors are deterministically generated via seeded Gaussian distributions on a unit sphere to benchmark engine scaling (at 10,000 vectors) without incurring prohibitive API costs or rate limits.

---

### 33. Why did we use `np.argpartition` instead of `np.sort`?
**Answer:** `np.sort` performs a full sort with $O(N \log N)$ complexity. `np.argpartition` locates the top-$k$ elements in $O(N)$ linear time without sorting the remaining $N-k$ elements. We then only sort the top-$k$ elements ($O(k \log k)$), resulting in significantly faster retrieval when $k \ll N$.

---

### 34. What is the score convention difference between Qdrant and NumPy for Euclidean distance?
**Answer:** In NumPy, we compute $d = \sqrt{\sum (v_i - q_i)^2}$, where smaller values represent closer points ($0$ is closest). Qdrant's `EUCLID` metric also uses distance where lower is closer, and sorts ascending. For Cosine, both systems treat higher scores (closer to $1.0$) as better.

---

### 35. What is the single most important takeaway from this project?
**Answer:** Vector search is grounded in geometric linear algebra: Cosine similarity is direction-sensitive while Euclidean distance is magnitude-sensitive. Normalizing vectors unifies these metrics, enabling efficient dot-product and Euclidean operations on unit hyperspheres, while graph indexes like HNSW scale these geometric principles from classroom 2D examples to multi-million production systems.
